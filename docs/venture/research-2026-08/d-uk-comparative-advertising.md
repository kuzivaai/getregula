# (d) UK comparative advertising: what a comparison table must satisfy

This is the best-evidenced section in this document. Both instruments are
primary, retrievable, and current, and one of them has been amended in a way that
most secondary write-ups will not yet reflect.

## The statute

**Primary**, retrieved 2026-08-17 from legislation.gov.uk: the **Business
Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008 (SI 2008/1276)**, in its
**revised** form, `valid_date` 2025-04-06, `modified_date` 2025-05-12, 29
provisions, extent United Kingdom.

Two definitions decide whether a comparison table is even in scope. Regulation 2:

> "comparative advertising" means advertising which in any way, either explicitly
> or by implication, identifies a competitor or a product offered by a competitor

> "advertising" means any form of representation which is made in connection with
> a trade, business, craft or profession in order to promote the supply or
> transfer of a product

**"By implication" is doing real work.** A table that never names a competitor but
describes one identifiably ("the main open-source scanner", "the venture-backed
platforms") is still comparative advertising. There is no version of a comparison
table that escapes regulation 4 by omitting names.

### Regulation 4: the nine conditions, quoted in full

Comparative advertising "shall, as far as the comparison is concerned, be
permitted **only when** the following conditions are met":

> (a) it is not misleading under regulation 3;
> (b) it is not a misleading action under section 225(4)(a) and section 226 of the
>     Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 or a misleading
>     omission under section 225(4)(a) and section 227 of that Act;
> (c) it compares products meeting the same needs or intended for the same purpose;
> (d) it objectively compares one or more material, relevant, verifiable and
>     representative features of those products, which may include price;
> (e) it does not create confusion among traders (between the advertiser and a
>     competitor, or between their trade marks, trade names, other distinguishing
>     marks or products);
> (f) it does not discredit or denigrate the trade marks, trade names, other
>     distinguishing marks, products, activities, or circumstances of a competitor;
> (g) for products with designation of origin, it relates in each case to products
>     with the same designation;
> (h) it does not take unfair advantage of the reputation of a trade mark, trade
>     name or other distinguishing marks of a competitor or of the designation of
>     origin of competing products;
> (i) it does not present products as imitations or replicas of products bearing a
>     protected trade mark or trade name.

These are **cumulative**. Failing one fails the comparison.

### The amendment most write-ups will get wrong

Regulation 4(b) as originally made cross-referred to the Consumer Protection from
Unfair Trading Regulations 2008. **The revised text cross-refers instead to the
Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024.** Verified independently at
legislation.gov.uk:

- **DMCCA 2024 s.226 (misleading actions)** came into force **6 April 2025**
  under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (Commencement
  No. 2) Regulations 2025, reg 2(1) and (3).
- **DMCCA 2024 s.227 (misleading omissions)** came into force **6 April 2025**,
  S.I. 2025/272, reg 2(1)(3).

That commencement date matches BPMMR's own `valid_date` of 2025-04-06, which is
two independent retrievals agreeing.

Three subsections of the new provisions bear directly on a comparison table, and
they are the ones that catch honest people:

> **s.226(2)** "the reference to misleading information includes a reference to
> information which, although true, is presented in a misleading way."

> **s.226(3)** "an overall presentation may be deceiving even if the information
> it contains is true."

> **s.227(4)** references to omitting information include providing information
> "(a) in a way that is unclear or untimely, or (b) in such a way that the
> consumer is unlikely to see it."

**True facts arranged to mislead are still misleading, and a caveat nobody will
see is still an omission.** A table of individually accurate ticks and crosses can
fail on its overall impression alone. A "verified on" date in 8pt grey under the
fold is squarely within s.227(4)(b).

### The substantiation burden, and it is reversed

Regulation 18(5) and (6):

> (5) In considering an application for an injunction the court **may require the
> person named in the application to provide evidence as to the accuracy of any
> factual claim** made as part of the advertising of that person ...
> (6) If, having been required under paragraph (5) to provide evidence ..., a
> person (a) fails to provide such evidence, or (b) provides evidence ... that the
> court considers inadequate, **the court may consider that the factual claim is
> inaccurate.**

The advertiser holds the evidential burden, and inadequate evidence is treated as
inaccuracy rather than as an open question. Regulation 18(7) adds that an
injunction may be granted "even where there is no evidence of proof of actual
loss or damage or of intention or negligence on the part of the advertiser".

### Enforcement and penalties

- Enforcement authorities (reg 2, 13): the **CMA**, every **local weights and
  measures authority** (Trading Standards), **DETINI**, and **GEMA**.
- Offence (reg 6): a trader commits an offence by engaging in misleading
  advertising under reg 3.
- Penalty (reg 7): summary conviction, a fine not exceeding the statutory maximum;
  **on indictment, a fine or imprisonment for a term not exceeding two years, or
  both.**
- Officers of a body corporate are personally liable where the offence was
  committed with their "consent or connivance" or is "attributable to any neglect"
  on their part (reg 8).
- Time limit (reg 10): three years from commission, or one year from the
  prosecutor's discovery, **whichever is earlier**.
- A due-diligence defence (reg 11) and an innocent-publication defence (reg 12)
  exist.

**Note the asymmetry that matters here.** The criminal offence at reg 6 attaches
to *misleading advertising* under reg 3. A breach of reg 4 alone (a comparison
that is honest but, say, not objectively verifiable) is not made a criminal
offence by reg 6; it is enforceable by injunction under regs 15 and 18 and by
undertakings under reg 16. Getting a comparison wrong is not automatically a
crime, and saying so is more accurate than the common shorthand.

## The CAP Code

**Primary**, retrieved 2026-08-17 from `asa.org.uk`, non-broadcast Code section 3.
The Code mirrors the statute and is what the ASA actually adjudicates against:

- **3.7** requires marketers to "hold documentary evidence to prove claims that
  consumers are likely to regard as objective". Note **before publication**, not
  on challenge.
- **3.32** comparisons with an identifiable competitor "must not mislead".
- **3.33** "must compare products meeting the same needs or intended for the same
  purpose".
- **3.34** "must objectively compare one or more material, relevant, verifiable
  and representative feature", which is the same wording as BPMMR reg 4(d).
- **3.35** must not create confusion between the marketer and its competitors.
- **3.36** protected geographical designations.
- **3.37** comparisons with an *unidentifiable* competitor must also not mislead.

**Stated limit on this retrieval:** the ASA page did not display a Code edition
number or a last-updated date in the content returned, so the rule numbering above
is current as at retrieval and is not pinned to a named edition. Anyone relying on
it should re-open the page and record the edition.

## The practical norms this implies for a comparison table

Reasoned from the two instruments above, not from a marketing source.

1. **Compare like with like, and say what "like" means.** Reg 4(c). A CLI scanner
   and a governance platform do not meet the same needs, so a single table
   spanning both is defective before any cell is filled in. If the table must
   span them, the axis of comparison has to be a need both genuinely serve.
2. **Every cell must be a feature that is material, relevant, verifiable and
   representative.** Reg 4(d) and CAP 3.34. "Verifiable" is the hard one: a
   reader must be able to check it. A cell whose evidence is "we looked" fails.
3. **Hold the evidence before publishing, not on challenge.** CAP 3.7, and reg
   18(5)-(6) makes inadequate evidence equivalent to inaccuracy.
4. **Date every competitor cell and record what was checked.** Not because a rule
   names dating, but because reg 4(d)'s "representative" and reg 3's "likely to
   deceive" are both assessed at the time the advertising runs. A cell that was
   true in April and is false in August is misleading in August. A dated,
   per-cell provenance note is the cheapest way to hold the evidence CAP 3.7
   requires.
5. **State where the competitor is stronger, in the same table and at the same
   visual weight.** This is the s.226(3) and s.227(4) point: a table listing only
   the dimensions on which you win is an "overall presentation" that deceives
   while every cell is true, and burying the counter-example is an omission
   "in such a way that the consumer is unlikely to see it". Under-weighted
   caveats are a named failure mode, not a stylistic preference.
6. **Do not denigrate.** Reg 4(f). "Cannot", "does not", "lacks" are factual;
   "bloated", "overpriced", "box-ticking" are not, and reg 4(f) is a separate
   condition from reg 4(a), so a denigrating comparison fails even when every
   fact in it is true.
7. **Omitting the name does not help.** Reg 2's "by implication", and CAP 3.37
   catches unidentifiable comparisons separately.

## Consequence for this project specifically

The project's own honesty rules already forbid claiming uniqueness without
exhaustively checking every competitor, and the ledger records three occasions
where a uniqueness claim failed that check. Regulation 4(d)'s "verifiable" and
CAP 3.7's "hold documentary evidence" turn that internal rule into an external
one with an injunction and a two-year maximum behind the related offence. A
comparison table is the single highest-risk artefact in the proposed roadmap, and
it is the one whose evidence requirements are clearest.
