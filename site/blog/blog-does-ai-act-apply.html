Does the EU AI Act apply to your AI app?

EU users can create a scope connection, but an API call or a user count cannot settle your obligations. Start with the system, the role your business holds, where it is supplied or used, and the exclusions that may apply.

Update, 16 September 2026: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 was published on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026. It changes the application dates for Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3, except Article 6(5), to 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for the Annex I product path. Article 50 generally applies from 2 August 2026, with a specific Article 50(2) transition for systems already on the market before that date. These dates do not settle whether a particular system or duty is in scope.

The short answer

Using AI and having users in the EU is usually enough to make the question live, but it does not settle it. Scope turns on the role you hold, what the system is for, and whether one of the Article 2 exclusions catches you. The rest of this page walks the actual test rather than assuming the answer.

Article 2(1) includes these three scope routes for providers and deployers:

  • Article 2(1)(a): Providers placing AI systems on the EU market or putting them into service, or placing general-purpose AI models on that market, whether established in the EU or a third country.
  • Article 2(1)(b): Deployers of AI systems who are established in, or located in, the EU.
  • Article 2(1)(c): Providers and deployers of AI systems established in a third country, where the output produced by the system is used in the Union.

The full provision also addresses importers, distributors, product manufacturers, authorised representatives and affected persons. An organisation outside the EU may therefore fall within scope, but the relevant role and any Article 2 exclusions must still be assessed.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 2(1). Published in the Official Journal of the European Union on 12 July 2024.

A decision tree

These five questions organise the facts a reviewer needs. They do not determine legal classification or establish compliance. If a fact is unknown, record the uncertainty and identify who can resolve it.

Work through these five questions in order. Each one narrows your position under the regulation.

1

Does your product use AI?

Compare the actual system with Article 3(1): consider its autonomy, objectives, inference from inputs and outputs that can influence physical or virtual environments. A library name or API call is evidence to investigate, not a legal determination. Record the system boundary and its intended purpose before reviewing the definition.

2

What connects the system to the EU?

Review where the system is placed on the market or put into service, where the deployer is established or located, and where outputs are used. Article 2(1)(a) to (c) sets out these routes. Check the relevant exclusions, including Article 2(6), (8), (10) and (12), rather than deciding scope from the number of EU users alone.

3

Are you the provider or the deployer?

Provider (Article 3(3)): the entity that develops an AI system or has it developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark. Deployer (Article 3(4)): an entity that uses an AI system under its authority, excluding personal non-professional use. Integrating a third-party model does not settle these roles: you may provide the resulting system while another organisation provides the model. See Section 4 for the facts to review.

4

What risk tier are you in?

The EU AI Act uses prohibited practices, high-risk classifications, and transparency duties rather than a universal four-tier clearance scheme. A system with no elevated indicator may still have duties under Articles 4 and 5 or other applicable law. High-risk areas include employment, essential services, education, law enforcement, and critical infrastructure, subject to Article 6 and its exceptions.

5

What are your obligations?

Possible Article 5 indicator: pause the affected use and obtain qualified review. Candidate high-risk: assess Article 6 and any exception before applying Articles 9–15. Article 50 indicator: confirm the applicable transparency duty. No elevated indicator: do not treat the result as legal clearance.

I am based in the US / UK / outside the EU — does it still apply?

It can. Article 2(1)(c) includes:

"providers and deployers of AI systems that have their place of establishment or are located in a third country, where the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union"

This route does not depend on having an EU office. It still requires the provider or deployer role and the relevant use of outputs in the Union, and must be read with Article 2's exclusions. Keep the territorial facts and the legal assessment separate.

Article 22 sets out an authorised-representative requirement for non-EU providers of high-risk systems. Assess it with the applicable Chapter III dates and transition rules, including the changes made by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 2(1)(c) and Article 22.

I use the OpenAI API: am I a provider or a deployer?

Calling an API does not determine your legal role. Assess the AI system you supply or use separately from the model it integrates. A business can provide an AI system built with a third-party model without training or fine-tuning that model.

Under Article 3(3), a provider develops an AI system or has it developed, then places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark. That definition can apply to an application built around a hosted model. Article 3(4) addresses an organisation using an AI system under its authority, excluding personal non-professional use. The relevant facts include what the system does, who develops or commissions it, whose name it carries, and who controls its use.

For example, a business developing and selling its own customer-support application that incorporates a hosted model may be the provider of that application. A business using a supplier's completed application under its authority may be its deployer. These examples illustrate the definitions; an actual role assessment still needs the deployment facts.

The model provider has a separate role. Article 3(63) defines a general-purpose AI model; it does not define the provider role. Article 53(1)(b) expressly addresses information that model providers make available to providers of systems integrating their models, subject to the exceptions in Article 53(2).

Article 25(1) adds specific routes to provider responsibilities for high-risk systems. Review these where you:

  • Put your name or trademark on a high-risk AI system already on the market or in service, taking account of Article 25(1)(a) and contractual allocations.
  • Substantially modify an existing high-risk AI system so that it remains high-risk, under Article 25(1)(b).
  • Change the intended purpose of an existing system so that it becomes high-risk, under Article 25(1)(c).

Keep role, classification and applicable dates as separate questions. For high-risk systems, provider obligations are set out in Article 16 and deployer obligations in Article 26. Article 50 assigns different transparency duties to providers and deployers. Neither an API call nor the absence of model training determines which duties apply.

Role section reviewed 16 September 2026 against Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 3(3), 3(4), 3(63), 16, 25(1), 26, 50 and 53, and Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. The Omnibus amendment changes the application dates for Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3: review the applicable date and transition provisions separately from the role definitions.

I am a solo founder with 3 users — do I really need to worry?

There is no general exemption simply because a business is small.

Article 2 does not provide a general exemption merely because a business is small. Its scope routes and exclusions still need to be assessed. A small customer count does not by itself establish either applicability or exemption.

Do not assume a small business will be ignored by enforcement authorities. Article 99(1), as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, requires proportionate penalties and consideration of smaller enterprises' interests and economic viability. That is not a promise that a particular infringement will go unenforced.

Article 62 requires Member States to provide SMEs and startups with priority access to AI regulatory sandboxes and to take the specific interests and needs of SME providers into account when setting fees and compliance costs. Recital 141 explicitly acknowledges that compliance costs should be proportionate.

The practical move is to document the system's intended purpose, roles, deployment context, and any detected indicators early. A tool can support that review but cannot establish that no obligations apply.

Omnibus status. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 entered into force on 27 July 2026. It changes the application dates for Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3, except Article 6(5), to 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for the Annex I product path. Other provisions and other applicable laws must be reviewed separately.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 2 and 62; Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, Article 1(38) and (40), and Article 4.
